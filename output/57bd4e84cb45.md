**Chaos magic**, also spelled **chaos magick**,[\[1\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECarroll2008-1)[\[2\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHumphriesVayne200517-2) is a [modern tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_\(supernatural\)#Modernity "Magic (supernatural)") of [magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_\(supernatural\) "Magic (supernatural)").[\[3\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChryssides201278-3) Emerging in England in the 1970s as part of the wider [neo-pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-pagan "Neo-pagan") and [esoteric subculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism#Later_20th_century "Western esotericism"),[\[4\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWoodman20032-4) it drew heavily from the occult beliefs of artist [Austin Osman Spare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Osman_Spare "Austin Osman Spare"), expressed several decades earlier.[\[3\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChryssides201278-3) It has been characterised as an [invented religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement "New religious movement"),[\[5\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECusackSutcliffe2017[[Category:Wikipedia_articles_needing_page_number_citations_from_May_2022]]\<sup_class="noprint_Inline-Template_"_style="white-space:nowrap;"\>&#91;\<i\>[[Wikipedia:Citing_sources|\<span_title="This_citation_requires_a_reference_to_the_specific_page_or_range_of_pages_in_which_the_material_appears.&#32;\(May_2022\)"\>page&nbsp;needed\</span\>]]\</i\>&#93;\</sup\>-5) with some commentators drawing similarities between the movement and [Discordianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discordianism "Discordianism").[\[6\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006233–238Duggan201496-6) [Magical organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magical_organizations "Magical organizations") within this tradition include the [Illuminates of Thanateros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminates_of_Thanateros "Illuminates of Thanateros") and [Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thee_Temple_ov_Psychick_Youth "Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth").
The founding figures of chaos magic believed that other [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult "Occult") traditions had become too religious in character.[\[7\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrury201186-7) They attempted to reduce the [symbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol "Symbol"), [ritualistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual "Ritual"), [theological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theological "Theological"), or otherwise ornamental aspects of these occult traditions, to leave behind a set of basic techniques that they believed to be the basis of magic.[\[8\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrury201186Hine200915-8)
Chaos magic teaches that the essence of magic is that perceptions are conditioned by beliefs, and that the world as it is normally perceived can be changed by deliberately changing those beliefs.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWoodman200315-16,_165,_201-9) Chaos magicians subsequently treat belief as a tool, often creating their own idiosyncratic magical systems and blending such different things as "practical magic, quantum physics, chaos theory, and anarchism."[\[10\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMeletiadis20232-10)
Scholar [Hugh Urban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Urban "Hugh Urban") has described chaos magic as a union of traditional occult techniques and applied [postmodernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernism "Postmodernism")[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClarke2004105–106-11) – particularly a postmodernist skepticism concerning the existence or [knowability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology") of objective truth,[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006240–243-12) positing that chaos magic rejects the existence of absolute truth, and views all occult systems as arbitrary symbol-systems that are only effective because of the _belief_ of the practitioner.[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006240–243-12)
## History
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")\]
### Origins and influences (1900–1982)
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Origins and influences (1900–1982)")\]
Further information: [Austin Osman Spare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Osman_Spare "Austin Osman Spare")
[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Austin_Osman_Spare.jpg/250px-Austin_Osman_Spare.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Austin_Osman_Spare.jpg)
Austin Osman Spare in 1904. His ideas formed the basis of chaos magic.
Austin Osman Spare's work in the early to mid 1900s is largely the source of chaos magical theory and practice.[\[13\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECarroll19878Siepmann201885-13) Specifically, Spare developed the use of sigils and the use of [gnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosis_\(chaos_magic\) "Gnosis (chaos magic)") to empower them.[\[14\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann201885-14)[\[15\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006231-15) Although Spare died before chaos magic emerged, he has been described as the "grandfather of chaos magic".[\[16\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVitimus2009115-16) Working during much the same period as Spare, [Aleister Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleister_Crowley "Aleister Crowley")'s publications also provided a marginal yet early and ongoing influence, particularly for his [syncretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretic "Syncretic") approach to magic and his emphasis on experimentation and deconditioning.[\[17\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine200945-17) Later, concurrent with the growth of religions such as [Wicca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicca "Wicca") in the 1950s and 1960s, different forms of magic became more common, some of which came in "explicitly disorganized, radically individualized, and often quite 'chaotic' forms".[\[18\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006233-18) In the 1960s and the decade that followed, [Discordianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discordianism "Discordianism"), the [punk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punk_subculture "Punk subculture") movement, [postmodernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernism "Postmodernism") and the writings of [Robert Anton Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Anton_Wilson "Robert Anton Wilson") emerged, and they were to become significant influences on the form that chaos magic would take.[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine200910-19)[\[20\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann201884-20)
During the mid-1970s chaos magic appeared as "one of the first postmodern manifestations of occultism",[\[14\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann201885-14) built on the rejection of a need to adhere to a "single, systematized convention",[\[21\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann201886-21) and aimed at distilling magical practices down to a result-oriented approach rather than following specific practices based on tradition.[\[22\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020767–768-22) An oft quoted line from [Peter Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_J._Carroll "Peter J. Carroll") is "Magic will not free itself from occultism until we have strangled the last astrologer with the guts of the last spiritual master."[\[23\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECarroll200846-23)
Peter J. Carroll and Ray Sherwin are considered to be the founders of chaos magic,[\[10\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMeletiadis20232-10) although Phil Hine points out that there were others "lurking in the background, such as the _Stoke Newington Sorcerors_".[\[24\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine20098-24) Carroll was a regular contributor to _[The New Equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Equinox "The New Equinox")_, a magazine edited by Sherwin, and thus the two became acquainted.[\[24\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine20098-24)[\[25\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDuggan201496-25)
In 1976-77 the first chaos magic organization [Illuminates of Thanateros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminates_of_Thanateros "Illuminates of Thanateros") (IOT) was announced.[\[26\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020762–763-26) The following year, 1978, was a seminal year in the origin of chaos magic, seeing the publication of both _Liber Null_ by Carroll and _The Book of Results_ by Sherwin – the first published books on chaos magic.[\[27\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDuggan201491-27)[\[28\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMeletiadis20238–23-28)
According to Carroll, "When stripped of local symbolism and terminology, all systems show a remarkable uniformity of method. This is because all systems ultimately derive from the tradition of Shamanism. It is toward an elucidation of this tradition that the following chapters are devoted."[\[29\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECarroll198730-29)
### Development and spread (1982–1994)
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Development and spread (1982–1994)")\]
New chaos magic groups emerged in the early 1980s – at first, located in [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire "Yorkshire"), where both Sherwin and Carroll were living. The early scene was focused on a shop in Leeds called _The Sorcerer's Apprentice_, owned by Chris Bray. Bray also published a magazine called _The Lamp of Thoth_, which published articles on chaos magic, and his _Sorcerer's Apprentice Press_ re-released both _Liber Null_ and _The Book of Results_, as well as issuing _Psychonaut_ and _The Theatre of Magic_.[\[30\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine20099-30) The "short-lived" _Circle of Chaos_, which included Dave Lee, was formed in 1982.[\[31\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020775-31) The rituals of this group were published by Paula Pagani as _The Cardinal Rites of Chaos_ in 1985.[\[32\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine200911-32)
[Ralph Tegtmeier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Tegtmeier "Ralph Tegtmeier") (Frater U∴D∴), who ran a bookshop in Germany and was already practicing his own brand of "ice magick", translated _Liber Null_ into German.[\[31\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020775-31) Tegtmeier was inducted into the IOT in the mid-1980s, and later established the German section of the order.[\[31\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020775-31)
As chaos magic spread, people from outside Carroll and Sherwin's circle began publishing on the topic. Phil Hine, along with Julian Wilde and Joel Biroco, published a number of books on the subject that were particularly influential in spreading chaos magic techniques via the internet.[\[33\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDuggan201495-33)
In 1981, [Genesis P-Orridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_P-Orridge "Genesis P-Orridge") established [Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thee_Temple_ov_Psychick_Youth "Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth") (TOPY).[\[34\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaddeley2010156-34) P-Orridge had studied magic under William S. Burroughs and Brion Gysin in the 1970s, and was also influenced by Aleister Crowley and Austin Osman Spare, as well as the [psychedelic movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelia "Psychedelia").[\[35\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann201890-35)[\[33\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDuggan201495-33) TOPY practiced chaos magic alongside their other activities, and helped raise awareness of chaos magic in subcultures like the [Acid house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_house "Acid house") and [Industrial music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_music "Industrial music") scenes.[\[36\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann2021283-36) Along with being an influence on P-Orridge, Burroughs was himself inducted into the IOT in the early 1990s.[\[37\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStevens2014ch._22-37)
### Pop culture: (1994–2000s)
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Pop culture: (1994–2000s)")\]
From the beginning, chaos magic has had a tendency to draw on the symbolism of [pop culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_culture "Pop culture") in addition to that of traditional magical systems; the rationale being that all symbol systems are equally arbitrary, and thus equally valid – the belief invested in them being the thing that matters.[\[38\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMorrison200316-25-38) The [symbol of chaos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_of_chaos "Symbol of chaos"), for example, was lifted from the fantasy novels of [Michael Moorcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Moorcock "Michael Moorcock").[\[39\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTENozedar200849-39)
Preluded by [Kenneth Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Grant_\(occultist\) "Kenneth Grant (occultist)") – who had studied with both Crowley and Spare, and who had introduced elements of [H.P. Lovecraft's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft "H. P. Lovecraft") fictional [Cthulhu mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_mythos "Cthulhu mythos") into his own magical writings[\[40\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTELevenda20138-40) – there was a trend for chaos magicians to perform rituals invoking or otherwise dealing with entities from Lovecraft's work, such as the [Great Old Ones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Old_Ones "Great Old Ones"). Hine, for example, published _The Pseudonomicon_ (1994), a book of Lovecraftian rites.[\[14\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESiepmann201885-14)
From 1994 to 2000, [Grant Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grant_Morrison "Grant Morrison") wrote _[The Invisibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invisibles "The Invisibles")_ for [DC Comics'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC_Comics "DC Comics") [Vertigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertigo_\(DC_Comics\) "Vertigo (DC Comics)") imprint, which has been described by Morrison as a "hypersigil": "a dynamic miniature model of the magician's universe, a hologram, microcosm or 'voodoo doll' which can be manipulated in real time to produce changes in the macrocosmic environment of 'real' life."[\[41\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMorrison200321-41) Both _The Invisibles_ and the activities of Morrison themself were responsible for bringing chaos magic to a much wider audience in the late 1990s and early 2000s, with the writer outlining their views on chaos magic in the "Pop Magic!" chapter of _A Book of Lies_ (2003)[\[38\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMorrison200316-25-38) and a [Disinfo Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disinformation_\(company\) "Disinformation (company)") talk.[\[42\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMetzger200298–115-42)
Morrison's particular take on chaos magic exemplified the irreverent, pop cultural elements of the tradition, with Morrison arguing that the deities of different religions ([Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes "Hermes"), [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(mythology\) "Mercury (mythology)"), [Thoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth "Thoth"), [Ganesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganesh "Ganesh"), etc.) are nothing more than different cultural "glosses" for more universal "big ideas"[\[41\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMorrison200321-41) – and are therefore interchangeable: both with each other, and with other pop culture icons like [The Flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Flash "The Flash"), [Metron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metron_\(character\) "Metron (character)"), and [Madonna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madonna_\(entertainer\) "Madonna (entertainer)").[\[41\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMorrison200321-41)
### Post-chaos magic: 2010s
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Post-chaos magic: 2010s")\]
Alan Chapman – whilst praising chaos magic for "breathing new life" into Western occultism, thereby saving it from "being lost behind a wall of overly complex symbolism and antiquated morality" – has also criticised chaos magic for its lack of "initiatory knowledge": i.e., "teachings that cannot be learned from books, but must be transmitted orally, or demonstrated", present in all traditional schools of magic.[\[43\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChapman200812-43) Innovations continue into the 2020s, as found in social media, fandoms, and webcomics.[\[44\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans202445-44)
## Beliefs, core concepts, and practices
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Beliefs, core concepts, and practices")\]
### Belief as a tool
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Belief as a tool")\]
The central defining tenet of chaos magic is arguably the idea that belief is a tool for achieving effects.[\[45\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020769f-45) In chaos magic, complex symbol systems like [Qabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qabalah "Qabalah"), the [Enochian system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enochian "Enochian"), [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology") or the _[I Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Ching "I Ching")_ are treated as maps or "symbolic and linguistic constructs" that can be manipulated to achieve certain ends but that have no [absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_\(philosophy\) "Universality (philosophy)") or [objective truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objectivity_\(philosophy\) "Objectivity (philosophy)") value in themselves. Religious scholar Hugh Urban notes that chaos magic's "rejection of all fixed models of reality" reflects one of its central tenets: "nothing is true everything is permitted".[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006240–243-12)
Both Urban and religious scholar Bernd-Christian Otto trace this position to the influence of postmodernism on contemporary occultism.[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006240–243-12)[\[46\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020764-46) Another influence comes from Spare, who believed that belief itself was a form of [psychic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_\(psychological\) "Energy (psychological)") that became locked up in rigid belief structures, and that could be released by breaking down those structures. This "free belief" could then be directed towards new aims.\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] Otto has argued that chaos magic "filed away the whole issue of truth, thus liberating and instrumentalising individual belief as a mere tool of ritual practice."[\[47\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtto2020771-47)
### Magical paradigm shifting
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Magical paradigm shifting")\]
[Peter J. Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_J._Carroll "Peter J. Carroll") suggested assigning different worldviews to the sides of a dice, and then inhabiting the associated particular random paradigm for a set length of time when its number is rolled. For example, 1 might be [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism "Paganism"), 2 might be [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism"), 3 might be [atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism"), and so on.[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban2006240–243-12)
[Phil Hine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Hine "Phil Hine") has stated that the primary task here is "to thoroughly decondition" the aspiring magician from "the mesh of beliefs, attitudes and fictions about self, society, and the world" that his or her ego associates with:
> Our ego is a fiction of stable self-hood which maintains itself by perpetuating the distinctions of "what I am/what I am not, what I like/what I don't like", beliefs about ones politics, religion, gender preference, degree of free will, race, subculture etc all help maintain a stable sense of self.[\[48\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHine2009[[Category:Wikipedia_articles_needing_page_number_citations_from_May_2022]]\<sup_class="noprint_Inline-Template_"_style="white-space:nowrap;"\>&#91;\<i\>[[Wikipedia:Citing_sources|\<span_title="This_citation_requires_a_reference_to_the_specific_page_or_range_of_pages_in_which_the_material_appears.&#32;\(May_2022\)"\>page&nbsp;needed\</span\>]]\</i\>&#93;\</sup\>-48)
### Cut-up technique
\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Chaos_magic&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Cut-up technique")\]
The [cut-up technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut-up_technique "Cut-up technique") is an [aleatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleatory "Aleatory") [literary technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_technique "Literary technique") in which a written text is cut up and rearranged, often at random, to create a new text. The technique can also be applied to other media such as film, photography, and audio recordings. It was pioneered by [Brion Gysin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brion_Gysin "Brion Gysin") and [William S. Burroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_S._Burroughs "William S. Burroughs").[\[49\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECran201686-49)
Burroughs – who practiced chaos magic, and was inducted into the Illuminates of Thanateros in the early 1990s – was adamant that the technique had a magical function, stating, "the cut-ups are not for artistic purposes".[\[50\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarris2017134-50) Burroughs used his cut-ups for "political warfare, scientific research, personal therapy, magical divination, and conjuration"[\[50\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarris2017134-50) – the essential idea being that the cut-ups allowed the user to "break down the barriers that surround consciousness".[\[51\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurroughs197428-51) Burroughs stated:
> I would say that my most interesting experience with the earlier techniques was the realization that when you make cut-ups you do not get simply random juxtapositions of words, that they do mean something, and often that these meanings refer to some future event. I've made many cut-ups and then later recognized that the cut-up referred to something that I read later in a newspaper or a book, or something that happened... Perhaps events are pre-written and pre-recorded and when you cut word lines the future leaks out.[\[51\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurroughs197428-51)
[David Bowie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bowie "David Bowie") compared the randomness of the cut-up technique to the randomness inherent in traditional divinatory systems, like the [I Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Ching "I Ching") or [tarot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot "Tarot").[\[52\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDoggett2011201-52)
Genesis P-Orridge, who studied under Burroughs[\[53\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEP-Orridge2003112-53) described it as a way to "identify and short-circuit control, life being a stream of cut-ups on every level. They are a means to describe and reveal reality and the multi-faceted individual in which/from which reality is generated."[\[54\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEP-Orridge2010132-54)
